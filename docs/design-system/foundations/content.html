Foundation

Content

Saturdaze talks like a friend who already sorted out Saturday: warm, calm and plain. It speaks to one parent at a time, usually on a phone, often with a kid asking what is happening tomorrow.

Overview

The reader is a parent deciding what the family does this weekend. They are busy, they know their kids better than any app, and they want the plan to be done. Every string should help them decide or get out of the way.

  • Lead with the answer. "Sunny Saturday for the lavender, a cloudy Sunday for the Rec Room." The reasoning comes second, if at all.
  • Use the family's words. Names (Eli, Mae), places (Terre Bleu, La Marina) and their routines (Swim, Church, Bath and books), not "member 2" or "event".
  • Say what happens next. "It sits around what was already planned." "Your family and weekends stay saved."
  • Saturdaze suggests, the family decides. "Saturdaze suggests weekend plans. You decide what to do."

Style

  • Sentence case everywhere: titles, buttons, tabs, labels, menu items. Capitalise names and places only: "Lock La Marina for Saturday lunch?", "Port Credit Memorial Park".
  • Address the reader as "you" and the family as "your family"; Saturdaze is "we" in legal and privacy copy, and otherwise mostly invisible.
  • Contractions are fine where they sound natural ("can't", "isn't"), but most mock copy reads well without them; do not force either.
  • No full stop on titles, buttons, labels, chips or single-phrase subs used as headings. Full stops on sentences in bodies, hints and errors.
  • Questions as titles only for confirmations ("Sign out?").
  • The middle dot " · " separates facts on one line: "Saturday 11:00 to 1:00 · 45 min drive". Never a pipe or a dash.
  • No exclamation marks. Not even for success.
  • Canadian spelling: favourite, colour, neighbourhood, licence (noun).

Buttons

A button label is a verb phrase that says what will happen, in two or three words. If it wraps at 360px it is too long.

Verb first; the object only when the context does not already name it.
  • Verb first: "Send reset link", "Add commitment", "Make primary".
  • Name the object when it is not obvious: "Regenerate Saturday" from the day header, "Remove photo" in admin. Inside a dialog titled with the object, the verb alone is enough: "Remove".
  • Match the title's verb: "Regenerate the weekend?" confirms with "Regenerate"; "Lock La Marina for Saturday lunch?" with "Lock it in".
  • The safe choice is honest about what it does: "Cancel", "Not yet", "Stay signed in", "Keep".
  • "Cancel" closes without changes; "Done" closes after a success. Never "OK", "Yes" or "Close" in a footer.

Use numerals for every number, including one to nine, so a glance at a timeline is enough. Numbers in lists and stats use tabular figures so columns line up.

The mocks mix two clocks: the timeline uses 24-hour time (18:30, 20:00) while dialog subtitles use a 12-hour clock without am or pm ("Saturday 11:00 to 1:00", "Workout 5:00"). Until the product settles it, keep each surface consistent with itself, and never mix both in one string.

Field errors sit under the field, start with the field's subject, and are joined to it with aria-describedby. On submit, an error summary at the top lists each one as a link to its field.

Empty states

Formula: what will be here + how it gets here + one action (or when it will happen on its own). An empty state is a promise, not an absence.

Nothing here yet

Plan this weekend and it shows up here on Monday, ready to rate.

Go to this weekend

Saturday and Sunday, drafted around the Browns

Swim, church and the workout stay put. Everything else gets planned around the weather and what the kids like.

Or wait for Friday at 6pm.

  • Title says what belongs here, or the good news ("Queue is clear").
  • Body says how it fills up, in the family's terms.
  • One action, the same verb as everywhere else. If nothing needs doing, say when it will happen ("Or wait for Friday at 6pm.").

Confirmations

Formula: the title asks the question with the real object; the subtitle says the consequence; the buttons repeat the verb. If the consequence is reassuring, say that too: people hesitate most over what they might lose.

Usage

Accessibility

  • Names that stand alone. Icon-only buttons say the action and the object: "Swap Lavender fields", "Why this: Lunch at La Marina", "Mae votes yes". Repeated links get unique names.
  • Visible words first. An aria-label starts with the visible label so voice control works (2.5.3). The day header's "Regenerate" is named "Regenerate Saturday", which works; its "Lock day" is named "Lock Saturday", which does not. Use "Lock day: Saturday".
  • Alt text says what the photo shows a family, not how it was taken. Admin alt text falls back to "Photo of Port Credit Memorial Park" when empty.
  • Travel legs read as sentences: "Travel: 45 minutes, 52 kilometres to Lavender fields", spelling out units that read badly aloud ("min", "km").
  • Plain language serves everyone: short sentences, common words, one idea per sentence. Aim for words a nine-year-old could read aloud.
  • Errors and status are announced, so their wording is heard without the screen: the message must make sense on its own.
