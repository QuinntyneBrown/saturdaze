# 33 · The plan, in plain words

Imagine a helper who knows your family. It knows hockey is at nine on Saturday, the baby naps at one, and nobody wants to drive for ninety minutes. It knows it might rain on Sunday. You tap one button, and it hands you a whole weekend that fits. That helper is Saturdaze. And planning is free.

So how does a free planner pay the bills? This video explains the Saturdaze business plan in plain words, as of October twenty twenty-six. At the end, we look at one big what-if: what changes if the seed money never comes.

## The problem

Planning a weekend with kids is a puzzle, and the pieces are everywhere. The events are on one website, the restaurants are on another, and the weather is in a third app.

And the puzzle is personal. The plan has to fit around practice, naps, car seats and a budget. Two grown-ups rarely want the same restaurant, so dinner ends up wherever is closest. And nothing remembers that you loved the zoo and hated the escape room.

So families spend hours and still do the same few things. Saturdaze puts the pieces in one place: fresh local events, places and restaurants, the family's commitments, and the forecast. Then it builds the plan for you.

There are about forty million families with children in Canada and the United States. About twelve million of them live in big cities with lots to do. Saturdaze starts with about one million in the Greater Toronto Area.

## Grown-up likes

The plan adds one new idea. Today, likes belong to the whole family. Next, every grown-up who signs in gets their own list of likes.

Then the planner can be fair. Places both grown-ups like come first. When tastes split, it takes turns, and it says so: Sam's pick this week. When a birthday party has the kids covered, it can suggest a coffee both parents like. And if any grown-up says no to a place, it's gone.

Kids keep the simple profile they have today. What they like helps plan the family's weekend, and nothing else.

## Five jars

Think of the money as five jars. Planning stays free, whatever jar we're talking about.

The first jar is Saturdaze Plus. Families who want more pay six ninety-nine a month, or fifty-nine ninety-nine a year. They get unlimited do-overs, turn-taking between grown-ups, budget planning and memories.

The second jar is bookings. When a family books tickets, a table or a class from inside the plan, the partner pays Saturdaze about three dollars.

The third jar is featured spots. A local place pays two to three hundred dollars a month for a spot marked Featured. It only shows up when it fits the family's ages, drive time and weather.

The fourth jar is Fan Reach. A place pays about a hundred dollars a month to tell grown-ups who liked it about something new. Only grown-ups who say yes get these alerts, and never more than two a week.

The fifth jar is Local Insights. Tourism boards and cities pay five hundred dollars a month or more to learn what families nearby want. For example: two thousand three hundred Mississauga families like Thai food, and there are only four Thai places nearby. They only ever see big groups, never a person.

The jars feed each other. Free plans bring families. Families leave likes. Likes make the business jars worth paying for. And that money keeps the planner free and the listings fresh.

## Year by year

The plan counts three years. In twenty twenty-seven, Saturdaze launches in Toronto and earns about one hundred and twenty-four thousand dollars.

In twenty twenty-eight, it grows to the rest of Ontario and three more Canadian cities, and earns about one point two seven million.

In twenty twenty-nine, it reaches its first American cities and earns about five point two six million dollars, from four hundred thousand families.

Here's the surprise. Families pay less than a fifth of that. Businesses pay the rest, through bookings, featured spots, Fan Reach and Local Insights. And Fan Reach and Local Insights only exist because grown-ups have their own likes.

And the careful version of the plan? If half as many families pay and book, twenty twenty-nine brings in about three point nine million instead.

## Spending and the seed

Before the money comes in, it has to go out. The plan spends about eight hundred and sixty thousand dollars in twenty twenty-seven and about two million in twenty twenty-eight, mostly on people. The team grows from four, to eight, to fifteen.

So the first two years lose about one point four four million dollars together. In twenty twenty-nine, Saturdaze makes a profit of about one point two four million.

That's why the plan asks for a seed of two million dollars. It covers the two losing years with about six months to spare. More than half of it builds the product. A quarter finds families. The rest goes to venue partners, privacy and the law.

## The road

Everything points at one date: the public launch in the Greater Toronto Area, in April twenty twenty-seven.

The seed closes in December twenty twenty-six. Featured spots start as a pilot in January. Grown-up likes get built from January to March. Then comes the launch. Saturdaze Plus follows in June, phone apps by November, and Fan Reach and Local Insights in January twenty twenty-eight. The rest of Ontario and three more Canadian cities follow that year. The plan breaks even, month by month, in March twenty twenty-nine, and then opens its first American cities.

By the end of the first year, the plan wants twenty-five thousand families, four in ten of them with two grown-ups signed in, and about twenty local places paying, on average.

## The never list

Saturdaze learns a lot about families, so the plan writes down what it will never do.

It never sells or shares what a person likes. Businesses see big totals, or reach people who said yes, through Saturdaze. It never uses children's data for offers, alerts or insights. And a paid spot never beats a grown-up's no, or a family's limits.

Each grown-up sees every like and every yes in one screen, and can download or delete them.

## The biggest risks

The first risk is that families plan for free and few pay. That's why Plus is only one jar. By twenty twenty-nine, the business jars bring in more than four dollars of every five.

The second risk is that local places won't pay until there's an audience. So listings start free to claim, and in the first year, prices follow results.

The third is trust. One privacy slip with people's likes could undo it all. The rules you just heard are the answer.

And big platforms could add family planning. Saturdaze bets on depth: the family's commitments, every grown-up's taste, and local partners.

## What if the seed never comes?

Now the what-if. The plan says it doesn't need a second, bigger round of money later. But it does need the first one. Here's what changes if the two million dollars never arrives.

First, the gap. In twenty twenty-seven, the plan's income covers about one seventh of its costs. Without the seed, there is no team of four, no big push to find families, and no launch of that size in April.

What you keep. The planner is already built and running. The Toronto listings are already stocked, and a computer helper can search the web for new events, places and restaurants. That's the hardest part, and it's done.

What you'd change. This part is our own thinking, not the plan's. Stay in one city. Start with the jars that need the least new building: featured spots, and bookings through partners' ready-made referral programs. Hold Fan Reach and Local Insights until there are enough families to make them worth paying for. And grow through invites, school councils and daycare groups, not paid ads.

What it costs. Time, first. Four hundred thousand families by twenty twenty-nine would slip by years. Second, the window. Big platforms could add family planning while Saturdaze grows slowly. Third, the founder. One person building, selling and answering every family is a lot, and growth waits on one person's hours.

So here's the verdict. Keep raising, but don't wait for it. Launch small in Toronto either way, and measure what the plan measures: how many families sign up, how many add a second grown-up, how many pay and how many book. If those numbers look like the plan's, they make the strongest case for the seed. If they don't, it's better to learn that on a small budget.

## Things to remember

- Saturdaze turns a family's commitments, tastes and the weather into a weekend plan, and planning is free.
- Every grown-up gets their own likes, so the plan works for the whole family.
- The money comes from five jars: Plus, bookings, featured spots, Fan Reach and Local Insights.
- Businesses pay most of it, and nobody's likes are ever sold.
- A two million dollar seed carries the plan to a profit in twenty twenty-nine. Without it, start small and let the numbers make the case.
